Valuation

IV15
“The stock price that provides a 15% annualized return 15 or more years into the future.” It is the price he waits for, not what the business is worth: “IV15 is not intrinsic value.”
IV8 / IV10 / IV12 / IV18
The same price at an 8%, 10%, 12% or 18% required return. A stock trading at its IV18 should return roughly 18% a year over a 15-year holding period.
Intrinsic Value
What the business is actually worth, using a 6% to 10% required return adjusted for risk. On his map it sits between IV8 and IV10, and generally between 1.5 and 2.25 times IV15, “with 2 or under my rule.”
P/IV15
Price divided by IV15, used in place of P/E. Below 1.0x, the price should deliver more than 15% a year; above 1.0x, less.
IVB
The yearly return to expect from buying at the current stock price.
Negative IV15
No stock price, not even $0.01, gives a 15% return. It does not mean the stock is worth less than zero; he leaves these alone as not investible.
Hybrid IV15
IV15 blends two runs of one model: a full multi-stage model with a terminal value, and the same model stopped at year 15 with a multiple on that year's owners' earnings. The mix depends on his confidence in each.
Stage 0
An extra opening stage added to the model for a young, fast-growing company just turning profitable, so its margins can build up over time.
Net Cash Per Share
Cash beyond what the business needs, minus debt, per share. For payments companies, debt is also adjusted for float, and long-term purchase obligations count as debt.

Owners' earnings and stock pay (Tragic Algebra)

Owners' Earnings (OE)
Profit that truly belongs to shareholders: net income with the reported stock pay expense replaced by its real cash cost. He also adjusts for normal tax rates, interest on excess cash, lease payments, and honest depreciation and amortization.
Tragic Algebra
His method for finding the true cost of stock-based compensation (SBC), worked out with Phil Clifton. Stock pay costs owners either through cash spent to offset dilution or through a stake that is “whittled down.”
Ω (Omega), Cash Cost of SBC
The true net cost of stock pay to shareholders, Ω = C + V. It replaces the reported expense G. Not the non-cash expense on the income statement.
C, Net Tax Payments
Cash the company pays to tax authorities when employee shares vest, less what employees pay in through option exercises and the employee stock purchase plan.
V, Value of Shares Delivered
The market value of the shares given to employees: V = T × (W + ΔS) / W, where T is buyback dollars, W is shares repurchased and ΔS is the change in share count.
ΔE
The fraction of reported net income that belongs to owners: (N + G − Ω) / N. The Nasdaq 100 pooled figure he found was about 83%. He prefers about ten years pooled, because single years swing widely.
Dilution Formula
Present value = next year's cash flow / [(1 + discount rate)(1 + dilution rate) − (1 + growth)]. His example: at 5% growth and a 10% discount rate, 1% yearly dilution cuts the multiple from 20x to 16.4x.
Buyback to Nowhere
Buybacks that only offset shares handed to employees, so cash is spent and the share count does not fall. Also the “buyback treadmill” or “hamster wheel.”
Tragic Tier
Companies whose cumulative stock pay cost exceeded reported SBC expense and net income put together, so owners' earnings are negative over the whole period.

Moat: AI Competitive Threat (AICT) tiers

Fortress
Strongest of five tiers. An AI aggressor, or a business with little to no realistic AI threat, often built into customers' regulatory or mission-critical systems. Few competitive weaknesses.
Castle
A strong moat and its own AI effort at true scale, with more uncertainty about how the business develops alongside AI-native rivals. Industry position is generally secure.
Chapel
Faces an acute AI threat but owns relevant AI, has real research and significant switching costs. The outcome is far more uncertain than for a Castle.
Stone
A real AI threat with little ability to adapt, or a low AI threat but strong competition and a shallow moat. Industry position may be seriously threatened.
Wood
Weakest tier. Any AI in the product is borrowed, not owned, with essentially no research of its own. Threatened with extinction in the long term, or stuck with low growth.
Using the Tiers
He qualifies them (“strong Chapel”, “low Castle”) and applies them outside software too. The tier shapes the growth and year-15 assumptions that go into IV15.

Ranking and the All Map

Composite Score
A 100-point score for ranking stocks against each other, built from three buckets. He uses it for relative value only, not to set what a stock is worth.
Shareholder's Bucket
How the company has treated owners: its Tragic Algebra standing, ΔE, the trend in stock pay, and how durable owners' earnings are.
Quality Bucket
The AICT tier, cash flow and float, gross margin and pricing power, net revenue retention, and the level and steadiness of return on invested capital.
Valuation Bucket
The multiple of price to IV15.
The All Map
His chart of covered stocks: P/IV15 on the vertical axis, AICT tier from Fortress to Wood across, and bubble size for the Composite Score.
Fat Pitch
The inner zone of the All Map: a rare opportunity worth swinging at. It weighs quality as well as price, so “not all IV18s are fat pitches” and a strong business can be one above IV15.
Just Outside / Out Field
The middle zone of the All Map holds pitches Just Outside, worth watching. Beyond it is the Out Field, which he avoids.
Full Position
His typical full position is about 6% of the portfolio. He holds less when disclosure is poor or when several holdings would move together.

Other adjustments

Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)
Owners' earnings divided by the capital at risk in the business. His guide to growth: “A company cannot outgrow its ROIC forever.”
Tax Normalization
Valuing a company at its own long-run tax rate, not a temporarily low one or a data vendor's standard rate.
Virtual Write-Down
An impairment of acquisition goodwill that he builds into his own valuation and charges against earnings over time, since reported goodwill is never amortized.
Purchase Obligations
Long, non-cancellable supply contracts that sit off the balance sheet. He treats them as debt and adds them to the capital base.
Float
Customer money a company holds before paying it out, as at payments and payroll companies.

AI and market terms

Supply-Side Gluttony
His name for a mania-driven investment boom in which capacity is built far ahead of demand. He calls it the cardinal sign of a bubble.
Tokenmaxxing
Employees told to use as many AI tokens as possible, which trains the company's models on their unpaid prompts. He treats the resulting demand as temporary.
The Bezzle
Galbraith's term for hidden losses that make everyone feel wealthier until they surface. Burry's version: temporary AI demand “financed and tallied as if it were permanent demand.”
Compression
Companies moving from buying AI from others toward running their own models, which shrinks outside demand. Seat compression is AI cutting the number of paid software seats.
One Lake One Life (OLOL)
Security firms that keep all customers' data in one protected pool: “if that one lake is breached, all the data is revealed.”
SDDD
Segregated and deception-defended data, the alternative he expects to replace One Lake One Life.
Mechanical Bid
Passive funds, retirement plans and buybacks that buy regardless of price, “that mechanical bid that always picks the market up.”
